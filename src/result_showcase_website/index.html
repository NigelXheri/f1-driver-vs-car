THE RESEARCH QUESTION

How much can we learn
from the stopwatch?

Race results belong to a whole system: driver, car, team, circuit and conditions. This project brings lap timings, race results, circuit characteristics and pit-stop records together to explore that system.

It finds measurable differences. It does not estimate a percentage of performance attributable to the driver versus the car. The evidence is observational, and the distinction matters.

THE CIRCUIT QUESTION

Distance matters.
Turn count tells less.

Across 42 circuit summaries, length and absolute lap time correlate at r = 0.756. Turns and seconds per kilometre have a weak linear association: r = 0.087.

This does not establish that corner geometry matters more: geometry was not modeled. One Wikipedia layout snapshot is used across historical seasons.

Selection, counts & interpretation

Only drivers reaching at least 90% of the race’s maximum recorded lap are included. First laps, matched pit laps and other pace exclusions still apply. Each driver-race-phase contributes one median. Survivorship, tyres, traffic, era, strategy and race-control events can affect this curve. No causal fuel effect or p-value is estimated.

Correction: the notebook says first rank-1 seasons span career years 2–10. Its table includes Rosberg in year 11; the correct observed range is 2–11.

What “rank” and “experience” mean

Ranks are reconstructed from available Grand Prix points across all drivers, using minimum rank for ties. Sprint points and official tie-break rules are not included. “Career year” counts calendar years since debut, including absences. The early-career test instead uses the first three participation seasons. Partial 2026 is excluded from career peak and early/later comparisons.

04 / THE EVIDENCE

A difference is visible.
Is it convincing?

Effect sizes tell us what was observed. Statistical tests ask how surprising that observation would be under a stated null hypothesis and its assumptions.

TEST 01 / WELCH’S TWO-SAMPLE t-TEST

Do rank-1 drivers peak
at a different mean age?

H₀: the two population mean driver-level peak ages are equal.
H₁: the means differ (two-sided).

Ten rank-1 drivers: mean 28.347, SD 3.240.
Five rank-2/3 drivers: mean 27.278, SD 2.364.

+1.069YEARS DIFFERENCE
0.726t · df = 10.806
0.4832p-VALUE

95% CONFIDENCE INTERVAL · DIFFERENCE IN YEARS

Do not reject H₀ at α = 0.05.

The interval includes zero and substantively different effects in either direction. This is inconclusive evidence, not evidence that the groups are equivalent.

Welch allows unequal variances. Independence and well-behaved group means still matter, especially with only five drivers in one group. The cohort is selected, so population inference is limited.

TESTS 02–03 / SPEARMAN + PERMUTATION

Does a strong start
signal later success?

Twelve drivers with observed debut seasons. First three participation seasons versus all later complete observed seasons.

H₀ for the permutation test: early and later outcomes are independent, making driver pairings exchangeable. The statistic measures monotonic association.

Neither comparison rejects its null at 0.05 after Holm correction. The moderate first correlation is an exploratory signal, not a validated forecast.

Lower rank numbers mean better results, so positive ρ means stronger early results tend to accompany stronger later results. Monte Carlo p-values are approximate; two tests share the correction family. No out-of-sample prediction was tested.

What a p-value actually says

A p-value measures how often a result at least as extreme would arise under H₀ and the test assumptions. It is not the probability that H₀ is true, the probability the finding is “due to chance,” or the size of an effect. “p-value” is the appropriate term here, rather than “p-score.”

Why no z-score or pooled Student’s test?

The notebook uses Welch’s t-test, with estimated standard errors and t-distributed reference uncertainty. A pooled Student’s t-test assumes equal population variances; Welch does not. A z-score standardizes a value but is not interchangeable with this t-statistic or its p-value. No z-test was performed, and none is invented here.

05 / UNDER THE BODYWORK

From raw records
to qualified findings.

The analysis preserves the source records and changes the comparison sample. A valid recorded duration can still be unsuitable for a pace question.

  1. 01

    Collect

    Jolpica calendars, results, laps and pit stops. Wikipedia circuit dimensions. FastF1 circuit and corner metadata.

  2. 02

    Inspect

    Parse durations; examine duplicates, missing positions, gaps and extreme laps. The EDA retains all 639,293 rows.

  3. 03

    Compare

    Select 577,990 pace laps across 587 races. Match teammates; normalize constructor pace; aggregate by circuit or race phase.

  4. 04

    Challenge

    Keep confounders visible. Use driver-level observations for career tests. Report effect sizes, null hypotheses and uncertainty.

Exact pace selection rules

Exclude lap 1, recorded pit-stop laps, candidate mass-delay race-laps (at least five drivers and at least 50% above twice the race median), and laps outside ±20% of the race median. Pit status before 2011 is unknown and retained; that does not imply no stops. Out-laps and many safety-car laps may remain. The 56 candidate events are heuristic flags, not verified race-control labels.

What remains unanswered

The isolated percentage contribution of car versus driver; the causal effect of fuel, tyres or pit stops; a universal peak age; validated career prediction; significance of the street/permanent contrast; a reliable all-time driver ranking. Repeated laps share drivers and races, so ordinary independent-lap significance tests would overstate sample information.
